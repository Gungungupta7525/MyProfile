When RAG actually helps a classifier

Retrieval-augmented generation is not automatically an improvement. What I learned building one on top of a TF-IDF news classifier.

  • ai
  • nlp

I added retrieval-augmented generation to a news classifier and it worked — partially, in a way I didn't fully understand at the time. This is what I understand now.

The baseline problem

Text classification with TF-IDF works by learning which words predict which label. Vectorise the article, compare against learned weights, output the highest-scoring label. It's fast, it needs no GPU, and for stable categories with predictable vocabulary it's genuinely good.

News is where it gets interesting, because the same event is described in completely different language on different days. "Shares tumble after profit warning" and "Shares nosedive as company flags weak guidance" share almost no vocabulary and both mean the same category. A bag-of-words model sees one of them. It does not see that they're the same thing.

What RAG actually changes

The important framing: RAG helps when it supplies information the model was never trained on. It does not help by making the model smarter at what it already knows.

For a TF-IDF classifier specifically, RAG adds a genuinely new channel of information — retrieved background the classifier has no way to derive from the article text. It can learn that an unfamiliar entity is related to a category because the retrieved context says so, not because the article happens to contain a keyword.

If RAG is just re-arranging information the model already had, skip it. If it adds information the model couldn't get on its own, it earns its cost.

Where it helped

The gain was real but concentrated, and that distribution is the whole story. It showed up on articles near the edge of the training distribution — new events, unfamiliar phrasing, entities the model had rarely seen. It was close to zero on articles already well covered by the training vocabulary.

A single averaged accuracy number would have hidden this completely. It would have said "RAG improved accuracy by X%" and I'd have been unable to tell anyone when it helped, which is the only useful part.

The experiment I should have run

I compared RAG-plus-TF-IDF against plain TF-IDF. That was the wrong experiment, because it can't tell you why it improved.

Three ablations I skipped, in order of how much I regret them:

  1. Retriever alone. Is the retrieved context relevant? I never measured retrieval quality independently of the classifier using it. One of those two was the bottleneck and I couldn't tell which.
  2. Context without retrieval. Does appending any extra text help, or specifically the relevant text? If random context works as well, your retriever isn't earning its complexity.
  3. A stronger non-RAG baseline. I never compared against a modern sentence embedding before building the retrieval layer. Quite possibly much of what RAG was doing for me, an embedding would have done more simply.

What I'd tell myself next time

  • Prove the retriever works first. Measure whether the right context is coming back, before you build anything on top of it.
  • Compare against embeddings, not just TF-IDF. The honest baseline for modern NLP isn't TF-IDF.
  • Report the distribution, not the mean. "It helps on out-of-distribution data" is useful. "It's 4% more accurate" is not.
  • Check that extra context isn't just extra tokens. Compare against a control with irrelevant text. If the control performs the same, the retrieval isn't doing anything.

The lesson wasn't that RAG doesn't work. It was that I built the interesting part before I built the measurement, and then couldn't tell how much of the result was real.
